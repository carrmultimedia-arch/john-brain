## 2\. The assignment, in one paragraph

Build one finished video from scratch for your own business, working Chapter 32's sequence in its own order — objective, audience, topic, hook, argument, call to action, script, environment, camera, lighting, audio, record, review, edit, caption, export, publish, measure — then hand back the video, the place it is published, and this checklist completed with one evidence line per step. The video is not judged on production polish. It is judged on whether each of the eighteen decisions was actually made, in order, and can be pointed at in the finished piece — which is exactly what Chapter 32 says the sequence is for: a video with no clear objective "can still look polished, but it rarely performs, because nothing in it is actually working toward anything specific."  
The steps are earned, not assumed. Seventeen of the eighteen taught steps map to chapters this programme scripts and teaches live (Section 5). The eighteen steps are the book's own order and none is optional: §32.3 only makes sense after §32.1 and §32.2, §32.4 assumes the audience §32.2 fixed, and §32.18 measures the objective §32.1 set.

## 6\. The nineteen steps

How to use the checklist. Each step has a standard (what the book asks for, in its own terms), a done when (the condition that closes the step, and the evidence line to hand back with it), and the chapter that taught it. Nineteen steps, one per TOC entry for Chapter 32, §32.1 through §32.19.

### Step 1 — Select the Objective · §32.1

The book's standard. "An objective is not a topic or a theme — it is a specific, concrete outcome." The test the book gives is measurement: "Get people to think we're good at what we do" is a feeling; "get three qualified inquiries from people who saw this and didn't already know us" is an objective, "because it is possible to look back after the fact and say honestly whether it happened."  
Done when. One sentence names the outcome this video is for, and a second says how it will be checked after publication. If the second sentence cannot be written, Step 1 is not finished — and neither is the video, whatever it looks like later.  
Evidence. The objective as written, in your own words.  
---

### Step 2 — Select the Audience · §32.2

The book's standard. The audience is "narrower than a business's overall target market — it is the specific segment of that market most likely to take the action defined by the objective." Being able to describe "who this person is, what problem or question brought them to this video, and what they already know or don't know coming in" is what selecting an audience means.  
Done when. You can name one person's situation — their question, and what they already know coming in — without describing your whole market. The book's reason is blunt: a video aimed at everyone "tends to genuinely reach no one."  
Evidence. Two or three lines describing that one person's situation, and the objective from Step 1 that it serves.  
---

### Step 3 — Select the Topic · §32.3

The book's standard. The topic is chosen "because it serves the objective for this specific audience, not because it is interesting to the business owner in isolation." It should be narrow: "How to choose a wedding videographer" is more useful than "everything about wedding videos." The book's check is to state the topic as a single sentence a real member of that audience might search for or ask out loud.  
Done when. The topic is one sentence, that sentence is something this audience would actually say, and you can say which of Step 1's outcomes it serves.  
Evidence. The topic sentence, as it would be asked.  
---

### Step 4 — Build the Hook · §32.4

The book's standard. The hook is the first few seconds and its only job is to earn continued attention; on nearly every platform "the majority of potential viewers decide whether to keep watching within the first three to five seconds." It typically states the audience's problem in their own language, promises a specific and credible payoff, or opens a small genuine curiosity gap — and it avoids "a slow introduction, a generic greeting, or a preamble." The book asks for this as its own writing task: draft several options and choose the strongest.  
Done when. At least three hooks are drafted, the strongest is chosen, and the chosen one contains no greeting and no preamble about what the video is about to cover. Nothing in the programme's own delivery chapters needs to be traded away for this: Day 2's Hook Construction is where it was taught.  
Evidence. The drafts and the chosen line.  
---

### Step 5 — Build the Argument · §32.5

The book's standard. The argument is the substantive core, and for a short-form video it is "usually built around one central point, not several." Covering three or four ideas in one short video "tends to dilute all of them," and needing several points is itself the signal that Step 3's topic was too broad or that the content wants to be a series. It is built in the audience's actual situation rather than in generic statements: "Specificity is what makes an argument feel credible and worth the viewer's time."  
Done when. One central point, stated in one sentence, delivered with one example or proof drawn from your own business rather than from general advice.  
Evidence. The central point in one sentence, and the example that carries it.  
---

### Step 6 — Build the CTA · §32.6

The book's standard. The call to action "should map directly back to the objective defined at the very start of this process." It is "specific and low-friction: it names one concrete action, not several competing ones," where "Follow for more" is the weak generic version and "Comment the word \[X\] and I'll send you the checklist" is specific and trackable. It also matches where the audience is: "a cold audience seeing the business for the first time is rarely ready for a high-commitment action like booking a call."  
Done when. The CTA names exactly one action, that action is the same outcome as Step 1's objective, and the ask is sized to what Step 2's audience already knows about you.  
Evidence. The CTA as it will be said, and the step or link it asks for.  
---

### Step 7 — Write the Script · §32.7

The book's standard. The script "should specify the order of ideas and the exact wording of anything that must land precisely, particularly the hook and the CTA" — it does not have to fix every word. Whatever form it takes, "it should be read aloud before moving to the recording stage," because a script that reads well on the page "can be awkward or unnatural coming out of a specific person's mouth."  
Done when. The script exists with Steps 4, 5 and 6 in their places, and it has been read aloud once, with the sentences that snagged rewritten rather than noted.  
Evidence. The script, and one line naming what the read-aloud changed.  
---

### Step 8 — Prepare the Environment · §32.8

The book's standard. The space is prepared deliberately: background checked for clutter and distracting motion, enough room to move if the shot calls for it, and outside noise sources — "HVAC systems, traffic, or other people" — controlled. If more than one session will be filmed for the same content, a continuity check on background, lighting and wardrobe belongs here. The book's cheap early catch is "a short walkthrough of the space with the camera's own preview" before the lighting and audio are set.  
Done when. The walkthrough is done on the camera's own preview, and the noise sources you could not remove are named so Step 11 can deal with them.  
Evidence. What the walkthrough changed, or a line saying it changed nothing.  
---

### Step 9 — Set the Camera · §32.9

The book's standard. Framing is chosen "based on the platform and format the finished video is headed for," which Step 1 and Step 2 already fixed; focus is "confirmed on the actual subject, not simply assumed from a glance at the screen," especially with autofocus that "can shift unexpectedly during recording if not locked"; and exposure is checked against the lighting rather than in isolation, because camera and lighting settings "genuinely depend on each other."  
Done when. Framing matches the format, focus is locked on the subject, and the exposure has been re-checked after Step 10, not before it.  
Evidence. The framing and focus setting, and the re-check after lighting.  
---

### Step 10 — Set the Lighting · §32.10

The book's standard. Lighting is set before the full take, not fixed afterwards: the subject's face "evenly and adequately lit without harsh shadows or blown-out highlights," and consistent with the tone the video is going for. Available window light "requires more active monitoring, since it changes throughout the day"; artificial light "requires an upfront setup and check against how it will actually render on camera, not just how it looks to the eye." The confirmation is "a quick recorded test clip, reviewed immediately."  
Done when. A test clip has been recorded and reviewed on a screen before the full take.  
Evidence. What the test clip showed.  
---

### Step 11 — Set the Audio · §32.11

The book's standard. Audio has an outsized effect on professionalism — "viewers are far more forgiving of imperfect visuals than they are of audio that is muffled, echoing, or full of background noise." Microphone placement must be close enough to capture clear, present sound, and any humming, echo or ambient noise is checked by ear. The test is a "short recorded test, played back through headphones rather than judged only by an on-screen level meter," because meters can show a technically fine signal while the recorded sound is compromised.  
Done when. A test has been played back through headphones, and any noise that cannot be removed has been accepted or solved deliberately rather than discovered in the edit.  
Evidence. What the headphone test showed.  
---

### Step 12 — Record · §32.12

The book's standard. Recording is "a deliberate process of capturing options, not a single attempt that must be perfect." The hook and the CTA — the two sections that most affect performance — get several takes with slightly different delivery. Longer scripts are recorded in sections rather than in one unbroken take, and "small technical checks — that the camera is still framed correctly, that the light has not shifted, that the microphone has not been bumped — should be repeated periodically rather than assumed to remain stable."  
Done when. The hook and the CTA have more than one take each, the session was recorded in sections, and the technical checks were repeated through it.  
Evidence. How many takes the hook and CTA have.  
---

### Step 13 — Review · §32.13

The book's standard. "Before breaking down the set, every recorded section should be reviewed in full, on a screen large enough to actually judge focus and framing accurately, and with real headphones to judge audio, not the built-in speaker of a phone or camera." The step exists to catch problems "while there is still time to simply record again": focus, exposure and audio, plus delivery — whether the hook lands, the argument is clear, the CTA is specific. "Any section that falls short on any of these should be re-recorded now, while the setup is still standing."  
Done when. Every section has been reviewed on a real screen and real headphones, and anything that fell short was re-recorded before the set was struck.  
Evidence. Which sections were re-recorded, or a line saying none needed to be.  
---

### Step 14 — Edit · §32.14

The book's standard. Editing assembles the strongest take of each section, trims dead space, corrects pacing and adds what the piece calls for — with the objective and audience from Steps 1 and 2 still guiding the decisions. The discipline the book names is "to watch the assembled draft all the way through as a first-time viewer would," because pacing problems are often invisible clip by clip; cutting "anything that does not directly serve the hook, argument, or CTA — even footage that looks good on its own — usually strengthens the finished piece." Any AI-suggested cut, caption or addition is "evaluated against whether it actually serves this specific video's objective and audience, not accepted simply because a tool suggested it."  
Done when. The draft has been watched once, in order, all the way through; at least one cut decision traces back to the hook, the argument or the CTA; and any AI assistance used was reviewed rather than accepted.  
Evidence. What the full watch-through changed.  
---

### Step 15 — Caption · §32.15

The book's standard. Captions belong on "essentially every video published to a modern platform," because a substantial share of viewers watch with sound off — and they widen reach for viewers with hearing impairments and viewers watching in a second language. Beyond accuracy, styling must be legible, high-contrast and in time with natural speech. Automated transcription gets a first pass quickly, but "names, technical terms, and any specific claim in the transcript should be checked against what was actually said."  
Done when. Captions are on and in sync, and every proper noun and specific claim in them has been checked by hand against the recording.  
Evidence. What the manual check corrected.  
---

### Step 16 — Export · §32.16

The book's standard. Export settings are chosen to match "the specific requirements of the platform the video is headed for" — resolution, aspect ratio, frame rate, file format — and the book asks for the platform's "current specifications" to be confirmed "directly rather than relying on memory, since these requirements do change over time." Then the exported file is reviewed once more in full, "ideally on the same type of device the audience is most likely to actually watch on," because compression can introduce artifacts or sync problems the editing software's preview did not show.  
Done when. The file was exported to the platform's current published specification, and watched back on the device the audience actually uses.  
Note on this step. No chapter of this programme teaches the platforms. §32.16 is the one step whose backing knowledge sits in the book's Chapters 23–28, which no Day scripts (Section 8). The step stands on the book's own instruction to check the platform's specification directly — which is the right instrument here in any case, because the specification changes and a course cannot carry it.  
Evidence. The specification checked and the device reviewed on.  
---

### Step 17 — Publish · §32.17

The book's standard. Publishing is more than the upload: it is "the caption or description text accompanying the video, the thumbnail if the platform uses one, any tags or categorization the platform supports, and the timing of when the video actually goes live." Timing follows when the audience is actually active, and "where a business has accumulated enough of its own platform history, that real data should take priority over general published benchmarks." Where the platform allows it, the CTA built in Step 6 is reinforced in the accompanying text — a pinned comment, a caption line, a link — so a viewer ready to act can act without remembering a verbal instruction.  
Done when. The video is live with its description, thumbnail and tags set, and Step 6's CTA is repeated somewhere the viewer can click it.  
Evidence. The published link, and where the CTA is reinforced in text.  
---

### Step 18 — Measure · §32.18

The book's standard. Measurement closes the loop to Step 1: "did this specific video actually accomplish what it set out to accomplish." Which metrics matter "depends entirely on the objective chosen at the start" — a video built to drive inquiries is measured primarily against inquiries generated, "not view count alone" — and measuring against a metric that was never the objective "produces a misleading picture of whether the video succeeded." Whatever the outcome, the lessons — what worked in the hook, whether the argument landed, whether the CTA drove the action — "should feed directly back into the next cycle of this same process, beginning again with a new objective."  
Done when. One honest measurement line is written against Step 1's objective, saying whether it happened, and one sentence names what the next cycle would change. A negative result closes this step exactly as well as a positive one; what fails the step is measuring something the objective never asked for.  
Evidence. The measurement line, placed beside Step 1's objective.  
---

### Step 19 — Practical Assignment · §32.19

The book's standard. §32.19 is the assignment step and has no text: the master TOC lists it and the body omits it, by the authoring rule recorded in Section 1\. Its content is this document — Steps 1 to 18 are the assignment, and this step is the submission itself.  
Done when. The three artefacts of Section 3 are in hand: the video, the place it is published, and the checklist with nineteen evidence lines — including Step 1's objective and Step 18's result readable side by side.  
Evidence. The submission set.
